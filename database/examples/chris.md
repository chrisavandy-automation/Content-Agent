I don't build our weekly KPI report anymore.

Claude does, but only because I built it by hand first.

The rule I followed: 10-80-10

Here's how I implement it...

1. The first 10%: prove it by hand

Before Claude touched anything, I filled the report in manually and wrote down every step, including:

- Which numbers judge the campaign: cost per lead, cost per booked call, cost per acquisition
- Which ones just help me diagnose it: hook rate, hold rate, CTR
- Where each number comes from: leads are counted in our CRM, spend comes from Meta, and I work out cost per lead myself, because Meta's own lead count isn't always accurate

I wrote my process down and turned it into an SOP.

2. The 80%: hand it over

I gave Claude the SOP and access to Meta and GoHighLevel. It turned my steps into a skill that runs every week and fills in the report for me.

3. The last 10%: check it

Before anything goes to the team, I sense-check it.

I actually ran a couple of manual reports side by side with Claude's, because how embarrassing would it be to hand over a KPI report with the wrong figures? 😅

The first 10% matters the most.
Automate a process you haven't proven and you just amplify a bad process...

## chris-05

How to build agentic workflows in Claude for marketing (my 5-step process + a free prompt):

Same process behind the agent that reads our sales and coaching calls every Monday, without me prompting it.

It pulls out the exact words clients use, so we write copy in their language.

1/ PICK ONE JOB
One you repeat every week, with the same steps each time. Can't describe it in one sentence? It's two jobs. Split it

2/ write the BRIEF
The agent's job description: who it is, what it's for, and its rules.
Does alone: e.g. pull last week's ad numbers
Asks first: e.g. anything that goes to a client
Never does: e.g. spend money or delete anything

3/ one SKILL per task
The steps in order, the software it uses, and what "done" looks like. Can't write the steps down? It's not ready to automate. Do it by hand first

4/ make it SELF-ANNEAL (every mistake becomes a rule)
Correct it once and it writes the fix as one line in a lessons file, then reads that file before every task. Mine have logged 60+ corrections in under 2 weeks (I'm fussy lol)

5/ put it on a ROUTINE
Pick a time, e.g. every Monday. Watch the first 5 runs and correct what it gets wrong. Then leave it alone

The free doc in the comments has the prompt that does steps 2 to 4 for you. It interviews you about the job, then builds the agent in about 10 minutes.

Plus example agents to test out

## chris-06
- Day 6/30 · 2026-10-06 · 3/1/0 · pillar 3 finding · none · value lane · hook insight · structure listicle
- Note: someone else's story; trailed

Jason Wojciechowicz raised a webinar offer from $32K to $50K.
Refunds dropped from 12 to 2.
Here are 6 of the changes he made:

At his event in London, he walked us through one of his clients funnels. It started with a weekly webinar, which sold a 3-day challenge. The challenge then sold the big offer: a $5K deposit followed by a booked call to pay the rest.

The catch was refunds, buyers were asking for their money back the very next day. He asked the room what we'd change to go from good to great. We threw answers at him for 5-10 minutes, and nobody got the full picture, me included.

1/ He removed "book a call"
Why? A day's wait is a day for buyer's remorse.
So a call centre rang buyers within 30 seconds of the deposit.

2/ He raised the price to $50K

3/ He added a cheaper done-with-you option
Didn't qualify for the $50K? You moved down instead of asking for your $5K back.

4/ He grew the slides from 171 to 381
Why? A bigger price needs more belief before the ask.

5/ He called everyone who showed up but didn't buy
A lot of them just had questions first.

6/ He ran retargeting ads all week
Only to people who'd shown up.

At $50K, the same funnel had just 2 refunds. He'd changed the whole journey, before and after the sale. As he told us that day, "Everything is a system, not a task."

- Day 7/30 · 2026-10-07 · 10/6/1 · pillar 3 finding · recent-past · emotion lane · hook question · structure story

Is AI making you dumber at learning?
Maybe. Here's how I make sure it doesn't...

I watched a video by Nick Saraev (The AI Automation Goat 💪) on how to learn with AI.
Learning happens in the struggle, and AI lets you skip it.
An AI explanation feels like understanding, but it isn't.
Like GPS leaving people with a poorer mental map, AI weakens the reasoning you'd use to get there yourself.

Memory is built by producing: recalling, predicting, explaining and trying things in the real world.

This study puts it into perspective well...

Group A re-read a document 4 times then got quizzed on it once, they remembered 83% after 5 minutes and 40% after a week.
Group B read it once and got quizzed 3 times, they remembered 71% after 5 minutes, 61% after a week.

After a week, Group A had forgotten over half of what they knew. 😮

So here is how I am using Claude to learn more efficiently...

I built a Claude plugin called Reps that contains these 10 roles (Credit to Nick!):

Interviewer: asks about you (your goal, your level, how you'll be tested) before teaching anything.

Mapmaker: breaks the subject into steps in dependency order, flagging where people get stuck.

Explainer: explains just the part you're stuck on, at your level. Then you redo the task without help.

Socratic questioner: asks you questions until you find the gap yourself, and never hands over the answer.

Examiner: scores your level with one question at a time, each harder, until you fail.

Checker: reviews something you made, and the steps behind it, finding what's wrong or a shorter route without rewriting it.

Listener: you explain it in your own words (spoken, drawn or written), and it compares that with the source.

Diagnostician: reads many past mistakes and names the one misunderstanding they share.

Sparring partner: role-plays a real situation under pressure, like an interview or a sales call, with a timer.

Clerk: turns your messy notes into a clean outline or flashcards, adding nothing.

## chris-09
- Day 9/30 · 2026-10-09 · 2/2/0 · pillar 2 how to · none · value lane · hook icp-callout · structure listicle
- Note: 'please stop' habit callout; trailed

If you paste the same prompt into Claude every week, please stop...


And start leveraging Claude skills + routines.

In August, I wrote a prompt to pull our weekly sales and client calls from Fathom.

Then I told Claude exactly how I wanted the data laid out, so we could write copy from it.
Why people bought. What nearly stopped them. The exact words they used.

It took a few rounds of iterating before we got it right.

Instead of saving the prompt and pasting it in again next week, I did this instead:

1/ PROMPT
Get it right by hand first. Run it, correct it, run it again.
Those corrections are the valuable bit. Right now they only live in that one chat

2/ SKILL
A skill is a saved set of instructions Claude follows step by step, every time.
Once the output was right, I asked Claude to turn the whole chat (the prompt and every correction) into one.
Try this at the end of your next good chat:
"Turn everything we just did into a skill I can reuse, including every correction I made."
Same steps, same layout, every week. No re-explaining

3/ ROUTINE
A routine runs the skill on a schedule, in the cloud. Your laptop can be closed.
The catch: it can only reach the apps you’ve connected (for us, Fathom and SharePoint), not files on your desktop.
(A loop does the same job, but only while your Claude session is open.)

I now run this skill on a routine weekly...

It pulls last week’s calls and updates one customer research doc in SharePoint.

Writing ads just got a whole lot easier and this is why Claude is my best friend 😂

In January 2023, I set out to sign 50 clients at £500 a month for my GoHighLevel automation business.

Instead, I spent the next 2 years getting good at building automations, but barely spent any time learning how to actually sell or market my business.

I built polished websites and workflows I was proud of. Most of them had no leads coming through.

Marketing brings people in.
Sales turns them into clients.
Delivery keeps them.

If I were starting an automation business again, I'd spend most of my time learning marketing and sales, and only polish a workflow once leads were coming in.

If you're building one right now, how is your week split?

ALT HOOKS:
- Marketing, then sales, then delivery. I learned it in reverse, and it cost me 2 years.
- I spent 2 years building polished automations for leads that never came.

## chris-13
- Day 13/30 · 2026-10-13 · 7/0/0 · pillar 3 finding · none · value lane · hook insight · structure listicle
- Note: breakdown of someone else's business; trailed

Brett Williams built a design business to over $100k a month.

No team. No ads. No sales calls.

Here's what he got right:

I heard his story a while back and it's stuck with me ever since.

He runs Designjoy, a design studio with exactly one person in it. Him.

But he didn't sell it like an agency.
He sold it like a Netflix subscription for design.

1/ One price, no quotes

$4,995 a month for unlimited design requests.
No proposal. No scoping call.
The client knows the price before they ever speak to him... and to be fair, they never really need to speak to him.

2/ One Trello board runs everything

You pay, you get added to the board, you add your requests in order of priority.
That's the whole client experience.
No inbox chaos.

3/ One request at a time, back in about 48 hours

This is the clever bit.
"Unlimited" sounds impossible for one person.
But he works through the board one request at a time, so the work never piles up.
The client gets speed and Brett gets a pace he can keep.

4/ No contracts, pause or cancel any time

Saying yes to $5k a month is a big ask.
Knowing you can stop next month is what makes it an easy one.

Fiverr and Canva already existed when he started, so cheap design was never the gap.
The packaging was.

Every choice on that list is what makes it easy for a client to buy and light for him to deliver.
Which is probably why his story has stuck with me for so long.

It's crazy how something so simple can scale so effectively.
