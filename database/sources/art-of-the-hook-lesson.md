# The Art of The Hook (LinkedIn course lesson, Jay Yang)

## What is a hook?
Advertising legend David Ogilvy once wrote, "When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar."
A hook is the first thing people see, hear, or read - and its only job is to stop the scroll.
On LinkedIn, it's the first 1-3 lines before someone has to click "more" to read the rest of your post.
If the hook doesn't land, nothing else matters, because nobody will read the rest of the post.
Good hooks get people to notice and pay attention. Bad hooks get ignored.
I try to make my hooks specific enough to get the right people's attention, but broad enough to capture as many of them as I can.

## Why do hooks matter?
If nobody notices your hook, nobody will read your post. If nobody reads your post, then nothing else matters. Your entire brand hinges on those first few seconds, and yet the vast majority of creators don't spend enough time on them.
The more experienced I get in content, the more I prioritize hooks above anything else.
A hook is also, in a way, the core idea or angle of a post. When you learn to write compelling hooks, you're really learning marketing. You're learning the key skill in life, which is, "Why anyone should care?"

## How do hooks work?
At its core, a hook has 2 parts: a callout and a promise.
1- A call-out is simply when you say who your post is for. Imagine you're at a party and there's a ton of loud sounds and noises, and yet someone whispers your name. You immediately snap your neck and turn to listen. Psychologists call this the "cocktail party effect". Our goal with our callouts is to harness the cocktail party effect and cut through all the noise.
A call-out can be as direct as: "If you're a stay-at-home mom who wants to make some side income while being present for your kids, this is for you."
2- A hook is a promise. People are literally paying their time and attention for your content. A hook is a promise that reading this post will provide more value than it extracts.
You want people to be aware of the promise, but you want to deliver on the promise towards the end of the post (to keep them reading).

## 10 Types of hooks
1. Announcement + context: "After 5 years of dreaming about launching my ice cream shop, I'm launching it this September!"
2. Authoritative sentence + how: "I sold my last company for $52 million. Here's the 3-step process I followed to actually get a profitable buyout:"
3. Authoritative sentence + lessons: "I sold my last company for $52 million. After 4 years of building, here are the biggest lessons I learned about being a founder:"
4. Declarative stance on a topic: "The easiest way to get wealthy in 2026 is by running an AI automation business."
5. Hot take / controversial opinion: "If you're not learning about AI, your job is going to be replaced by someone who is."
6. Moment in time: "In April of 2023, I moved away from everyone I knew."
7. Thought-provoking question: "Would you rather work 5 days in-office for 200k, or fully remote for 150k?"
8. Vulnerable statement: "For the past 12 months, we've had a churn problem."
9. Unique insight: "After analyzing 30,000 of the top LinkedIn posts, we found out that 90% of them are less than 20 lines of text."
10. ICP Callout: "If you're in your 20s and don't know what to do with your life:"

## My process for finding and creating hooks
My hook creation process follows Google's 70/20/10 innovation rule.
1. 70% of your time should be spent on things that are already proven in your own content or other people's. (Reference the 10 examples; use Stanley to do competitor research on hooks, see Module 2.)
2. 20% of your effort should go to stuff that's promising but less proven. I call this adjacent. If you see something in a completely different niche that's working, you may want to adopt that concept.
3. 10% should be spent on new, big ideas: trying out completely crazy things.

## Exercise: Keep a swipe-file
A swipe file is a folder to keep things that you want to remember. I have a swipe file of every hook that captured my attention and stopped my scroll. When I need inspiration to write my own hook, I open my swipe file of hundreds of hooks. You should start one too.

## Hook best practices
- Curiosity gap: give just enough context where the reader is missing key information; hint at value by adding an "unexpected twist" ("I applied to 150+ jobs and got rejected from every single one… until I started implementing this trick I learned from a LinkedIn executive:").
- Be specific: instead of "3 lessons on the job search", say "3 lessons I learned about landing a job after 200+ applications, 30 interviews, and 10 job offers".
- Add credibility: use numbers ("I will increase your sales by 135% in the next 2 weeks"); borrow credibility from famous figures / brands ("Tony Robbins once said"); use first-person language (I, my, me).
- Use recent language: "I just learned about", "I recently learned about".
- Speak to your avatar: "If you've been struggling with".

## Battle-testing hooks
Before I write even the body of my post, I "battle test" my hooks: I write 3-5 hooks for every post, compare them, choose the best one, and go with that.
1. That trains your mind to think of many different hooks.
2. It allocates your time and attention properly. A better hook = a better idea for the post, which means the post itself will be better.

Summary: hooks matter, a lot; allocate your time accordingly. Study hooks, study your competitors' hooks, use the examples and tips. Double down on what works.
